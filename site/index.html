The question

How much of the value of what a farm produces actually reaches the farm — and what determines the answer?

11.8¢ of every dollar spent on domestically produced food reached U.S. farms in 2024, down from 12.1¢ in 2023. USDA ERS Food Dollar Series, 2026 revision.

That series is carefully built and it answers the question it was built for: the aggregate division of the national food dollar. But read only that far and you would conclude farmers lost ground in 2024 — and ERS publishes the decomposition that shows why stopping there misleads.

The farm share of groceries rose. What moved the national figure was where people ate: away-from-home spending grew 4.2% to $1.27 trillion while at-home spending grew 1.4% to $901 billion, and a farmer sees about seven cents of a restaurant dollar against about eighteen and a half cents of a grocery dollar. "The farm share is falling" is therefore, in large part, a sentence about restaurant habits — not about farmers losing ground in a market they actually sell into. Those two readings call for entirely different responses, and one national ratio cannot tell them apart.

The same release carries a second warning. The 2026 revision broadened the definition of food to include bottled water, soft drinks, coffee and tea, whose supply chains use few farm commodities; that change alone restated 2024 from 12.3¢ to 11.8¢. Half a cent turned on a decision about what counts as food. Headline measures are constructions, and knowing how they are built is the first requirement for saying anything durable with them.

What no national ratio can do is answer the question in the form producers actually ask it. Does certification raise the share I realise, or only my costs? If I sell through a food hub rather than a terminal market, does more of the retail price come back to me? When credit is tight, do I take the first offer because I cannot afford to hold the crop?

Those are questions about mechanisms operating between the farm gate and the consumer. Answering them needs operation-level data observed over time — not a national ratio.

What the programme does

Much of the necessary data exists, but as operational registers: administrative databases agencies maintain to do their jobs, in the present tense. A register tells you who is certified today, which markets are operating now. Entry, exit and survival — the dynamics that decide whether a mechanism works for producers — are not what a register is for, and mostly not what it preserves.

01

Reconstruction

Where an agency publishes its register over time, the raw material for a panel exists but the panel does not. USDA AMS publishes monthly snapshots of the Organic INTEGRITY Database; nobody has assembled them into an operation-spell panel with identity resolved consistently across snapshots. That resolution is the hard part, and it is the contribution.

02

Preservation

Where an agency does not archive its register, the record is being lost continuously and cannot be recovered at any price. The USDA Local Food Directories are current-state listings with no public historical archive. This programme therefore begins snapshotting on the day it is founded, before it has published anything — because the value of an archive is set entirely by when it started.

Three strands

Each is a mechanism standing between farm and consumer.

C

Certification

Whether third-party certification changes what producers realise, who enters — and, the question the literature mostly skips, who stops. register · USDA Organic INTEGRITY

I

Intermediation

How the farm-gate-to-retail spread is divided, how it moves, and whether channel choice changes the producer's share. Platform and contract intermediation — where the take rate is a marketing margin set by a firm rather than discovered in a market — belongs here. registers · AMS Market News · Local Food Directories · SNAP retailers

$

Credit

Whether credit access determines a producer's capacity to hold product, invest in certification, or bargain at all. CRA, FCA and NCUA use incompatible definitions that must be reconciled before they can be read together. registers · FFIEC CRA · FCA · NCUA

U.S. measurement is the spine. A named comparative strand carries cocoa-economy and international certification work, where marketing-chain institutions differ sharply enough to discipline claims about what is mechanism and what is merely American.

Standards

Nine commitments, in summary. Everything public, under an open licence, with no embargo. Reproducible by a stranger from the README alone. Provenance logged on every fetch. Numbers interpolated from a pipeline stats file, never typed into prose. Limitations written before conclusions. Corrections public and versioned. Registers archived without their contact details, because a monthly public file of tens of thousands of small farms' personal telephone numbers is not the same act as an agency's search box. And completeness outranks automation: where an agency offers both a convenient machine route and a complete one, the complete route is the archive's capture and the convenient one is at most a labelled backstop.

And the one that governs this page: nothing is claimed before it exists. No dataset, identifier, download count, adoption, or publication status appears in any programme document until it is real and independently verifiable.

This page is a summary. The charter is the governing document — it states each standard in full, along with the programme's scope boundaries, what it is not, and its governance.
